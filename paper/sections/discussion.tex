\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the ablations show}
\label{sec:discussion-ablations}

The ablations attribute the estimator's behavior to its three design
choices.

\paragraph{Weighting.} Of the three, weighting has the largest effect, and
it has that effect where the survey design is informative, which in this
paper means the \scf{}. The \scf{}'s list sample oversamples wealthy
households. Without the weighted bootstrap the estimator is six times worse
on net-worth marginal fit within the donor survey (4.3 times on the 2025
wave), and on the population view its imputed 99th percentile is 1.9 to 2.3
times the holdout's (1.8 on the 2025 wave), against 0.70 to 0.77 with the
weights. That effect is larger than the gap between the full estimator and
every competing method except OLS and the random hot deck, whose draw also
ignores the weights.

On the \cps{} components task, no effect of weighting exceeds its dispersion
across seeds (dividend $W_1$ rises by 36, sd 47, without it). We read that
as a sign that the \cps{} person weights carry little information about
these outcomes. Among adults of the same age, sex, and employment income, an
adult's weight says little about their interest or dividend income, so a fit
that ignores the weights learns nearly the same conditional distributions.
We infer this from the null result and did not test it directly. An analyst
cannot always know in advance which of the two situations a survey is in,
which is the argument for making the weighted fit the default and the
unweighted fit an explicit request. The weighted fit has a cost of its own
on the population view, where it understates the 99th percentile and the
unweighted fit overstates it.

\paragraph{Gating.} Within the estimator, the gates are what keep the share
of zeros right. With its gates off, the estimator misses the dividend zero
share by 7.2 percentage points instead of 0.6 (4.3 with chaining off as
well), and the \scf{} debt zero share by 7.2 instead of 2.0. The mechanism
we propose, a forest that pools leaf samples and so draws a spike at zero
too often, is reproduced by a synthetic test. It is not measured in these
runs, which record the size of a miss and not its sign. Methods that donate
or resample observed values hold the zero share without a gate. On the
population view's joint metrics every gates-off row sits within one floor
standard deviation of its gated counterpart.

\paragraph{Chaining.} Chaining matters where the targets must satisfy a
relationship among themselves, and there most of its effect comes from that
relationship. In the two-target task the unchained ablation stays within
half a floor standard deviation of the full estimator. Fitting four
balance-sheet items independently costs 5.8 AUC points (4.9 on the 2025
wave). When each item is imputed on its own, each has the right
distribution, but for a given household the imputed net worth no longer
equals the imputed assets minus the imputed debt. A metric that looks at one
variable at a time cannot detect that, because nothing is wrong with any one
variable. On the 2022 wave only the classifier test, which sees all of a
household's variables together, shows the cost. On the 2025 wave one
single-variable metric also moves: the unchained net-worth $W_1$/sd rises
from 0.048 to 0.062.

Computing net worth from three independently imputed components removes the
5.8-point cost. Chaining those three scores 1.2 points lower still, which is
about half a floor standard deviation and holds on all ten paired seeds. On
this balance sheet, computing net worth from the identity did better than
having the forests learn it as a fourth chained target, by 1.7 points (0.8
floor standard deviations; 2.5 points on the 2025 wave).

Where the data do not call for a design choice, keeping it on costs little.
On the 2022 wave the unweighted ablation is slightly ahead on the \scf{}
debt zero share, and it is ahead on the OpenML suite, whose weights are
uniform.

\subsection{Comparison with matching}
\label{sec:discussion-matching}

The results do not show that the estimator outperforms a hot deck that uses
the weights. On the metrics of this paper the two are level within noise,
and the differences in the estimator's favour are about one floor standard
deviation.

Within a survey, a hot deck that draws its donor among the nearest in
proportion to weight equals the full estimator on marginal fit, where the
nearest-neighbour hot deck is 3.7 times worse on \scf{} net worth. That gap
comes from choosing the donor without the weights, not from matching as
such. On the 2022 \scf{}, whose weights are informative, the same draw
without the weights closes none of it (about a quarter on the 2025 wave). On
the \cps{} the draw closes it with or without the weights. All four hot
decks preserve the share of zeros, because they donate observed values.

On the population view the order of the hot decks reverses. The weighted
random hot deck is weaker than the nearest-neighbour one on the classifier
test and, on the 2022 wave, in the extreme tail (on the 2025 wave at
production scale its tail is the better of the two). The full estimator's
AUC is lower than both hot decks' in the minimal profile (by 2.1 and 2.9
points). At production scale it is lower by 0.7 and 1.6 points as specified,
and by 2.4 and 3.3 with net worth derived. Those margins run from a third of
the floor's seed-to-seed standard deviation to one and a half times it, and
on the 2025 wave at scale the nearest-neighbour hot deck's AUC is the lower
of the two by 0.3 points. The constrained hot deck, which has the best
marginal fit on several within-survey columns, was not run on the population
view, so its joint scores are unknown.

Two readings of this near-parity are possible, and the experiments do not
separate them. The first is that the methods are close on these tasks. The
survey tasks have three to ten predictors and at most four targets. Whether
the estimator gains on a hot deck when there are more predictors, and close
donors become scarce, is not tested by any task here. The second is that the
metrics are weak where a difference would show. The within-survey metrics
score each imputed variable on its own and say nothing about how it varies
with the predictors. On the population view, every candidate carries the
same demographic difference between the two surveys, which raises every
candidate's AUC and leaves less room for the methods to differ: the full
estimator's AUC is 0.73 and 0.76, against a sampling floor of 0.50. And
energy distance separates only OLS from the rest. Conditional fit can be
tested directly within a survey, where the receivers' true values are known
(Section~\ref{sec:future}). We did not run that test.

\subsection{What the evaluation shows}
\label{sec:discussion-evaluation}

Three points concern how imputed files are evaluated.

First, marginal metrics do not separate a draw that ignores the predictors
from methods that use them. An unconditional weighted draw from the donor
marginal has the best pinball loss on the \scf{} as coded (read under
receiver weights it ties the full estimator) and ties for second of the ten
methods on the OpenML suite, although it carries no conditional structure.
On the four within-survey $W_1$ columns it ranks sixth or seventh of
thirteen. At production scale the classifier separates it from the holdout
at an AUC of 0.968, against the full estimator's 0.761.

Second, the joint metrics miss an error confined to the tail. All three
tied the unweighted ablation with the full estimator while its imputed
net-worth 99th percentile was double the holdout's. Energy distance and
coverage are computed on subsamples of 2,048 points, of which the households
in the inflated tail are a small share, and we attribute their tie to that.
The classifier uses every row and tied too, and we did not vary the cap. We
found the inflation by comparing weighted quantiles directly, and the
uncapped tail metrics were added to the harness as a result. We take from
this that an evaluation of heavy-tailed microdata should include a statistic
that looks at the tail directly, with a sampling floor to show how much that
statistic moves by chance (the floor's own q99 ratio averages 1.10).

Third, the fragility diagnostic is read against the truth and is not to be
minimized. The target is the held-out data's own exposure to reweighting.
The one method with far lower exposure (OLS, at factors of 7 to 11 below the
truth) has it because, as implemented here, its draws do not reach realistic
tails. On the within-survey task where we ran it, the diagnostic did not
separate the weighted fit from the unweighted one.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{One pair of surveys.} The population-view experiment compares one
pair of surveys. The two differ in their income concepts, in the ASEC's
topcoding, and in income reference years, which span 2021 to 2024 across the
pooled vintages. That difference is shared by all methods, and it limits how
close any candidate can come to the floor. The experiment separates the
unchained ablation and the unconditional draw from the rest. The differences
between the estimator and the hot decks are within about one and a half
floor standard deviations. Scoring one candidate against several surveys at
once belongs to the harness's own development \citep{ghenis2026popdgp}.

\paragraph{Subsampled joint metrics.} Energy distance and coverage run on
weight-proportional subsamples of 2,048 points per side. The classifier test
and the tail metrics use all rows.

\paragraph{The extreme tail.} The full estimator understates the extreme
tail on the population view: its imputed net-worth q99 is 0.70 to 0.77 of
the holdout's. On the 2022 wave the nearest-neighbour hot deck shows the
same shortfall (0.75 to 0.76), the weighted random hot deck a larger one
(0.52 to 0.60), and the unconditional marginal draw none (1.09 to 1.12). The
cause is therefore not specific to forest draws, and this experiment does
not isolate it. On the 2025 wave the estimator's ratio is 0.63 to 0.64, and
donor reuse lifts the nearest-neighbour hot deck's to 2.85 at production
scale. The full estimator's results are therefore not uniformly favourable,
and the tail metrics are what show it.

\paragraph{Baselines as implemented.} The OLS and quantile-regression
baselines are converted to samplers through a 99-level grid, which truncates
draws beyond the 1st and 99th conditional percentiles. The \microimpute{}
forest is run as shipped. It draws each value from a ten-level conditional
quantile grid, which reaches no further than the 9th and 91st conditional
percentiles and over-draws those two levels. Its constructor takes no seed,
so its forest and its sampler run on one fixed internal seed in every cell.
Its paired-seed dispersion therefore carries no forest noise, and its
contrasts with the full estimator mix the gate with the sampler. Every
target of a row is read at the same quantile index, which bears on its joint
score with four chained targets (AUC 0.817).

\paragraph{Defaults and the choice of tasks.} The comparisons hold shared
forest backends and default hyperparameters fixed and do not tune each
method per task. The four ablations are exact controls, and the comparisons
across method families are defaults against defaults. That comparison is not
symmetric. The estimator's defaults were set with the 2022 \scf{} results in
view, and the targets were chosen to exercise its gates and chaining, while
the baselines were not tuned to these tasks. The 2025 wave is the only
\scf{} data that no design decision saw.

\paragraph{Arms evaluated on part of the tasks.} The constrained hot deck
took thirteen minutes per split at the population view's 20,000 receivers,
against seconds for every other method, and was not run there. The gates are
tested on targets whose sign classes are all common, not on a rare class.

\paragraph{Dollar basis.} The population view does not put the two surveys'
incomes on a common dollar basis: the \scf{}'s are in survey-year dollars
and the ASEC's in each income year's. Re-basing the 2022 donor to 2025
dollars, with nothing else changed, lowers the full estimator's net-worth
q90 ratio at production scale from 1.00 to 0.89
(Appendix~\ref{app:scf2025}), so tail ratios on this view carry that offset.
On the 2025 wave the ratio is \rep{scale-q90-candidate:2025}, and the
re-basing accounts for part of that fall.

\paragraph{Variation outside the seeds.} Three sources of variation sit
outside the seed-to-seed spread used throughout. Every \scf{} run uses the
first of the survey's five implicates and none uses its replicate weights,
so the spread carries neither imputation nor sampling variance. A pure
change of dollar units moves the forest-based methods' ten-seed $W_1$ means
by up to \rep{units:forest-max} percent (Appendix~\ref{app:scf2025}), which
is the size of some differences read above. And at production scale the full
estimator's AUC varies more across seeds than any other non-OLS row's (a
standard deviation of 0.026, and 0.048 on the 2025 wave, against 0.015 or
less). One seed in each wave accounts for most of that (0.829 and 0.889),
and we have not examined why.

\paragraph{Single imputation.} We evaluate single imputations
distributionally and do not propagate imputation uncertainty in the
multiple-imputation sense \citep{rubin1987multiple}. For the population
statistics that microsimulation consumes, the distributional view is the
relevant one, but inference from imputed files would require the fuller
treatment.

\subsection{Open questions and future work}
\label{sec:future}

\paragraph{A direct test of conditional fit.} Within a survey the receivers'
true values are known, so conditional fit can be scored directly: with a
classifier that tries to tell receiver rows carrying their true values from
the same rows carrying imputed ones, or with a proper score of each method's
predictive distribution at each row. Either would show whether the
near-parity between the estimator and the weighted random hot deck belongs
to the methods or to the metrics used here.

\paragraph{Harder tasks.} The survey tasks have three to ten predictors and
at most four targets, and the donor and receiver are either the same survey
or two surveys of one population. Tasks with more predictors, more targets,
and larger differences between donor and receiver would test whether the
comparison with matching changes.

\paragraph{Results we have not explained.} Six results are reported without
an explanation: the unweighted ablation's lower classifier AUC in all four
combinations of profile and wave; the \microimpute{} forest's q99 ratio,
which is nearer 1 than the full estimator's; the shortfall at the 99th
percentile that the full estimator shares with two hot decks; the one seed
in each wave with a much higher AUC at production scale; the interaction of
gating and chaining on the dividend zero share; and the part of the fall in
the q90 ratio on the 2025 wave that re-basing does not account for.

\paragraph{Runs not made.} The constrained hot deck was not run on the
population view, the subsample cap was not varied, and the fragility
diagnostic was not run on a cross-survey file, which is the setting that
motivates it. No run uses the other four \scf{} implicates or the replicate
weights, and the gates were not tested on a rare sign class. The zero-share
metric records the size of a miss and not its direction.
