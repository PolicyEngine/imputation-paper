\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The results attribute the estimator's behavior to its design choices with a
regularity worth stating plainly. \emph{Weighting is the dominant choice
wherever the design is informative}: on the \scf{}, whose list sample
oversamples wealthy households, removing the weighted bootstrap costs a
factor of six on net-worth marginal fit within the donor survey and inflates
the imputed 99th percentile on the population view by a factor of 1.9 to
2.3. That effect is larger than the gap between the full estimator and every
competing method except OLS. On the \cps{} components task, whose person weights carry little outcome
information, weighting matters far less: removing it raises dividend $W_1$
by about a third and leaves the other metrics within seed noise. An estimator cannot know in
advance which regime it is in, which is the argument for weighting by
construction rather than by option. \emph{Gating carries the zero mass}:
with its gates off, the same estimator misses the dividend zero share by 7.2
percentage points instead of 0.6, and the \scf{} debt zero share by 7.2
instead of 2.0, because a forest that pools leaf samples over-draws an atom
at zero. On the population view's joint scores every gates-off row sits
within one floor standard deviation of its gated counterpart.
\emph{Chaining matters only where the targets are jointly constrained, and
there most of its effect is the constraint}: invisible with two loosely
coupled targets, fitting four balance-sheet components independently costs
5.8 AUC points, but computing net worth from three independently imputed
components recovers that, and chaining those three adds 1.2 points more.
Only the omnibus classifier sees any of it, because a balance sheet that
does not add up has perfectly reasonable marginals. The practical reading is
that an identity among targets should be imposed rather than learned. Where
a choice does not bind, its cost is small: the unweighted ablation is slightly
ahead on \scf{} debt zero share and on the uniform-weight OpenML suite.

Against matching, what the estimator adds is on the joint, and it is modest.
A hot deck that draws its donor among the nearest in proportion to weight
equals the full estimator on within-survey marginal fit, where the
nearest-neighbour hot deck is 3.7 times worse on \scf{} net worth. That gap
belongs to the nearest-donor rule, not to matching. On the \scf{}, whose
weights are informative, the hot deck that uses them to choose the donor
closes it and the constrained hot deck, which does not, leaves it open; on
the \cps{}, whose weights carry little outcome information, both close it.
All three hot decks preserve the zero mass by donation. On the population
view the order reverses: the weighted random hot deck is the weaker of the
two on the classifier score and on the extreme tail, and the full
estimator's AUC is the lowest of the three in the minimal profile (by 2.1
and 2.9 points) and, with net worth derived, at production scale (by 2.4
and 3.3). Those margins are one to one and a half times the floor's
seed-to-seed standard deviation.

The evaluation findings are as load-bearing as the method findings, and one
of them corrected this paper's own harness. Marginal metrics saturate: with
weak predictors, an unconditional weighted draw from the donor marginal sits
at or near the top on pinball loss and Wasserstein distance
--- on the OpenML suite it ranks second of the eight methods --- while
carrying no conditional structure at all; at production scale the classifier separates it
almost perfectly (AUC 0.968 against 0.761). But the joint metrics have their
own blind spot: capped pairwise geometry tied the unweighted ablation with
the full estimator while its imputed net-worth 99th percentile was double the
holdout's, because the discrepant mass is a sliver of standardized pairwise
space. We caught this by comparing weighted quantiles directly, added the
uncapped tail block to the harness, and report it as a general lesson:
sample-geometry evaluations of heavy-tailed economic microdata need an
explicit tail axis, with a sampling floor to calibrate its noise (the floor's
own q99 ratio wobbles to 1.10). The fragility diagnostic adds the
complementary caution: the target is the truth's own tail exposure, not
minimal exposure, and the one method that minimizes it (OLS, at factors of 7
to 11 below truth) does so by failing to generate realistic tails at all.

Several limitations bound the claims. First, the population-view experiment
runs one view pair: the common \cps{}--\scf{} offset (differing income
concepts, ASEC topcoding, and income reference years spanning 2021 to 2024
across the pooled vintages) is shared by all methods but bounds how close any
candidate can come to the floor. A multi-view instantiation --- one candidate
scored simultaneously against \cps{}, \sipp{} \citep{census2023sipp}, and
\psid{} \citep{psid2023} views --- would tighten the identification and is
the production release-gate use of the harness; we leave it to future work
(\psid{} access requires registration), since the two-view experiment
already identifies the joint-structure differences this paper is about. Second, the joint-geometry blocks still run
on weight-proportional subsamples (2,048 points per side); the tail block and
the C2ST, which use all rows, are the axes least affected. Third, the full
estimator understates the extreme tail on the population view: its imputed
net-worth q99 is 0.70 to 0.77 of the holdout's. The nearest-neighbour hot
deck shows the same shortfall (0.75 to 0.76), the weighted random hot deck a
larger one (0.52 to 0.60), and the unconditional marginal draw none
(1.09 to 1.12), so the cause is not specific to forest draws; this
experiment does not isolate it. The tail block keeps the shortfall visible,
and it is a caution against reading the full estimator's wins as uniform.
The OLS and quantile-regression baselines are additionally converted to
samplers through a 99-level grid, truncating draws beyond the 1st and 99th
conditional percentiles. Fourth, the \microimpute{} forest is run as
shipped. It draws each value from a ten-level conditional quantile grid,
which reaches no further than the 9th and 91st conditional percentiles and
over-draws those two levels, and its constructor takes no seed, so its
forest and its sampler run on one fixed internal seed in every cell. Its
paired-seed dispersion therefore carries no forest noise, and its
contrasts with the full estimator mix the gate with the sampler. Fifth,
comparisons hold shared forest backends and default hyperparameters fixed
rather than tuning each method per task; the four ablations are exact
controls, the cross-family comparisons are defaults-versus-defaults. Sixth,
two arms are evaluated on part of the surface only. The constrained hot
deck's assignment problem is too large at the population view's 20,000
receivers, so it is absent there, and the gates are tested on targets whose
sign classes are all common, not on a rare class. Finally, we evaluate single
imputations distributionally and do not propagate imputation uncertainty in
the multiple-imputation sense \citep{rubin1987multiple}; for the population
statistics microsimulation consumes, the distributional view is the relevant
one, but inference from imputed files would require the fuller treatment.
