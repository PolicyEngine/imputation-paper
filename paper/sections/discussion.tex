\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The ablations attribute the estimator's behavior to its three design
choices. \emph{Of the three, weighting has the largest effect, where the
design is informative}, which in this paper means the \scf{}. Its list
sample oversamples wealthy households. Without the weighted bootstrap the
estimator is six times worse on net-worth marginal fit within the donor
survey (4.3 times on the 2025 wave), and on the population view its imputed
99th percentile is 1.9 to 2.3 times the holdout's (1.8 on the 2025 wave),
against 0.70 to 0.77 with the weights. That effect is larger than the gap
between the full estimator and every competing method except OLS and the
random hot deck, whose draw is weight-blind too. On the \cps{} components
task, whose person weights carry little outcome information, no effect of
weighting exceeds its dispersion across seeds (dividend $W_1$ rises by 36,
sd 47, without it). An estimator cannot know in advance which regime it is
in, which is the argument for weighting by construction rather than by
option. The weighted fit has a cost of its own on the population view, where
it understates the 99th percentile and the unweighted fit overstates it.
\emph{Within the estimator, gating carries the zero mass}: with its gates
off, it misses the dividend zero share by 7.2 percentage points instead of
0.6 (4.3 with chaining off as well), and the \scf{} debt zero share by 7.2
instead of 2.0. The mechanism we propose, a forest that pools leaf samples
over-drawing an atom at zero, is reproduced by a synthetic test and is not
measured in these runs, which record the size of a miss and not its sign.
Methods that donate or resample observed values hold the zero share without
a gate. On the population view's joint scores every gates-off row sits
within one floor standard deviation of its gated counterpart.
\emph{Chaining matters where the targets are jointly constrained, and
there most of its effect is the constraint}. In the two-target task the
unchained ablation stays within half a floor standard deviation of the full
estimator, while fitting four balance-sheet components independently costs
5.8 AUC points (4.9 on the 2025 wave). Computing net worth from three
independently imputed components recovers that, and chaining those three
scores 1.2 points lower still, which is about half a floor standard
deviation and holds on all ten paired seeds. On the 2022 wave only the
omnibus classifier registers any of it, because a balance sheet that does
not add up can leave every marginal intact; on the 2025 wave the unchained
net-worth $W_1$/sd also rises, from 0.048 to 0.062. On this balance sheet,
imposing the identity did better than learning it, by 1.7 points (0.8 floor
standard deviations; 2.5 points on the 2025 wave). Where
a choice does not bind, its cost is small: on the 2022 wave the unweighted
ablation is slightly ahead on \scf{} debt zero share, and it is ahead on the
uniform-weight OpenML suite.

Against matching, what the estimator adds is on the joint, and it is within
about one floor standard deviation.
A hot deck that draws its donor among the nearest in proportion to weight
equals the full estimator on within-survey marginal fit, where the
nearest-neighbour hot deck is 3.7 times worse on \scf{} net worth. That gap
is weight-blind donor choice, not matching. On the 2022 \scf{}, whose
weights are informative, the same draw without the weights closes none of
it (about a quarter on the 2025 wave); on
the \cps{}, whose weights carry little outcome information, the draw closes
it with or without them. All four hot decks preserve the zero mass by
donation. On the population view the order reverses: the weighted random
hot deck is weaker than the nearest-neighbour one on the classifier score
and, on the 2022 wave, on the extreme tail (on the 2025 wave at production
scale its tail is the better of the two). The full estimator's AUC is lower
than both hot decks' in the minimal profile (by 2.1 and 2.9 points). At
production scale it is lower by 0.7 and 1.6 points as specified, and by 2.4
and 3.3 with net worth derived. Those margins run from a third of the
floor's seed-to-seed standard deviation to one and a half times it, and on
the 2025 wave at scale the nearest-neighbour hot deck's AUC is the lower of
the two by 0.3 points. The constrained hot deck, which has the best marginal
fit on several within-survey columns, was not run on the population view,
so its joint scores are unknown.

Two findings concern evaluation, and one of them changed this paper's own
harness. First, two marginal scores do not separate an unconditional draw
from conditional methods. An unconditional weighted draw from the donor
marginal posts the best pinball loss on the \scf{} as coded (read under
receiver weights it ties the full estimator) and ties for second of the ten
methods on the OpenML suite while carrying no conditional structure. On the
four within-survey $W_1$ columns it ranks sixth or seventh of thirteen. At
production scale the classifier separates it from the holdout at an AUC of
0.968, against the full estimator's 0.761. Second, the joint blocks have a
blind spot of their own. All three tied the unweighted ablation with the
full estimator while its imputed net-worth 99th percentile was double the
holdout's. For the two capped blocks we attribute that to the small part of
standardized pairwise space the discrepant mass occupies, but the
classifier uses every row and tied too, and we did not vary the cap. We
found the inflation by comparing weighted quantiles
directly and added the uncapped tail block to the harness. We take from it
that sample-geometry evaluations of heavy-tailed microdata should carry an
explicit tail axis, with a sampling floor to calibrate its noise (the
floor's own q99 ratio averages 1.10). The fragility diagnostic adds a
related caution: the target is the truth's own tail exposure, not minimal
exposure, and the one method that minimizes it (OLS, at factors of 7 to 11
below truth) does so because, as implemented here, its draws do not reach
realistic tails. On the within-survey task where we ran it, the diagnostic
did not separate the weighted fit from the unweighted one.

Several limitations bound the claims. First, the population-view experiment
runs one view pair: the common \cps{}--\scf{} offset (differing income
concepts, ASEC topcoding, and income reference years spanning 2021 to 2024
across the pooled vintages) is shared by all methods but bounds how close any
candidate can come to the floor. A multi-view instantiation (one candidate
scored simultaneously against \cps{}, \sipp{} \citep{census2023sipp}, and
\psid{} \citep{psid2023} views) would tighten the identification and is
the production release-gate use of the harness; we leave it to future work
(\psid{} access requires registration). The two-view experiment identifies
the chaining and unconditional-draw differences; the differences between the
estimator and the hot decks are within about one and a half floor standard
deviations. Second, the joint-geometry blocks still run
on weight-proportional subsamples (2,048 points per side); the tail block and
the C2ST, which use all rows, are the axes least affected. Third, the full
estimator understates the extreme tail on the population view: its imputed
net-worth q99 is 0.70 to 0.77 of the holdout's. On the 2022 wave the
nearest-neighbour hot
deck shows the same shortfall (0.75 to 0.76), the weighted random hot deck a
larger one (0.52 to 0.60), and the unconditional marginal draw none
(1.09 to 1.12), so the cause is not specific to forest draws; this
experiment does not isolate it. On the 2025 wave the estimator's ratio is
0.63 to 0.64, and donor reuse lifts the nearest-neighbour hot deck's to
2.85 at production scale. The tail block keeps the shortfall visible,
and it is a caution against reading the full estimator's wins as uniform.
The OLS and quantile-regression baselines are additionally converted to
samplers through a 99-level grid, truncating draws beyond the 1st and 99th
conditional percentiles. Fourth, the \microimpute{} forest is run as
shipped. It draws each value from a ten-level conditional quantile grid,
which reaches no further than the 9th and 91st conditional percentiles and
over-draws those two levels, and its constructor takes no seed, so its
forest and its sampler run on one fixed internal seed in every cell. Its
paired-seed dispersion therefore carries no forest noise, and its
contrasts with the full estimator mix the gate with the sampler. Every
target of a row is read at the same quantile index, which bears on its
joint score with four chained targets (AUC 0.817). Fifth,
comparisons hold shared forest backends and default hyperparameters fixed
rather than tuning each method per task; the four ablations are exact
controls, the cross-family comparisons are defaults-versus-defaults. That
comparison is not symmetric. The estimator's defaults were set with the
2022 \scf{} results in view, and the targets were chosen to exercise its
gates and chaining, while the baselines were not tuned to these tasks. The
2025 wave is the only \scf{} data no design decision saw. Sixth,
two arms are evaluated on part of the surface only. The constrained hot
deck took thirteen minutes per split at the population view's 20,000
receivers, against seconds for every other method, and was not run there,
and the gates are tested on targets whose
sign classes are all common, not on a rare class. Seventh, the population
view does not put the two surveys' incomes on a common dollar basis: the
\scf{}'s are in survey-year dollars and the ASEC's in each income year's.
Re-basing the 2022 donor to 2025 dollars, with nothing else changed, lowers
the full estimator's net-worth q90 ratio at production scale from 1.00 to
0.89 (Appendix~\ref{app:scf2025}), so tail ratios on this view carry that
offset. On the 2025 wave the ratio is \rep{scale-q90-candidate:2025}, and
the re-basing accounts for part of that fall. Eighth, three sources of
variation sit outside the seed-to-seed spread used throughout. Every \scf{}
run uses the first of the survey's five implicates and none uses its
replicate weights, so the spread carries neither imputation nor sampling
variance. A pure change of dollar units moves the forest-based methods'
ten-seed $W_1$ means by up to \rep{units:forest-max} percent
(Appendix~\ref{app:scf2025}), which is the size of some differences read
above. And at production scale the full estimator's AUC varies more across
seeds than any other non-OLS row's (a standard deviation of 0.026, and
0.048 on the 2025 wave, against 0.015 or less). One seed in each wave
accounts for most of that (0.829 and 0.889), and we have not examined why.
Finally, we evaluate single
imputations distributionally and do not propagate imputation uncertainty in
the multiple-imputation sense \citep{rubin1987multiple}; for the population
statistics microsimulation consumes, the distributional view is the relevant
one, but inference from imputed files would require the fuller treatment.
