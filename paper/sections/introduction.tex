\section{Introduction}

Microsimulation models of tax and transfer policy require individual records
that span domains no single survey covers
\citep{bourguignon2006microsimulation, sutherland2013euromod}. In the United
States, the Current Population Survey (\cps{}) observes employment and income
but not wealth; the Survey of Consumer Finances (\scf{}) observes wealth in
detail but lacks the \cps{}'s coverage and program detail; administrative tax
records observe income components precisely but only for filers, and with
narrow demographics. Model builders bridge these gaps with imputation: fit a
conditional model of the missing variables on a donor survey, then draw
values for the receiver's records
\citep{little2002statistical, dorazio2006statistical}.

Two properties of imputation for microsimulation distinguish it from generic
missing-data problems. The first it shares with any imputation between
surveys: both files are weighted samples. Each record stands in for a
different number of population units, and the quantity of interest is a
population distribution, not a sample one. An imputation model fit to
unweighted records learns the sample's conditional distribution, which
differs from the population's exactly when the design (or a prior
calibration) correlates weights with outcomes. The second is specific to microsimulation
data: the imputed file is not an endpoint. It is reweighted afterwards, when
it is calibrated to administrative margins
\citep{deville1992calibration, hainmueller2012entropy}, filtered to
subpopulations, or stressed under reforms. An imputation that looks
acceptable under the weights it shipped with can therefore fail under weights
it never saw. A rare donor record carrying an extreme value at near-zero weight
barely moves totals under the shipped weights; a later reweighting that
assigns it mass turns it into a distortion of the population total. We refer to such records
as \emph{landmines}. Section~\ref{sec:results-cps} applies a reweighting
stress test for them to a within-survey task (Table~\ref{tab:fragility}),
where it does not separate the weighted fit from the unweighted one.

This paper presents and evaluates the imputation estimator of
\populace{},\footnote{\populace{} was first released under the name
Populace. Artifact and release identifiers created before the rename keep
the legacy \texttt{populace} prefix, and the reproducibility pins in the
data-availability section reference the repository at a pre-rename commit.}
\policyengine{}'s open-source microdata stack \citep{microcosm2026}. The
estimator is a quantile regression forest with three design choices, each
aimed at one of the problems above. A \emph{weighted bootstrap} puts survey
weights into the training data of each forest, so that the forests learn
weighted conditionals. \emph{Sign gates} classify each record's sign class
(negative, zero, positive) before magnitudes are modeled, to keep the shares
of zero and negative values in variables that have them, which are common
in economic microdata \citep{mullahy1986specification, lambert1992zero}.
\emph{Sequential chaining} conditions each imputed variable on
the ones already drawn, to carry joint structure among targets through the
imputation \citep{raghunathan2001multivariate, vanbuuren2011mice}. The interface
enforces the first choice: fits are weighted by default, and an
unweighted fit must be requested explicitly.

We test the estimator on wealth and capital income, which have the features
these choices are aimed at. From the \scf{} we impute household debt and net
worth and, in a larger experiment, the balance-sheet items behind net worth
(financial assets, nonfinancial assets, and debt). Within the \cps{} we
impute interest and dividend income. Most adults have no dividend income,
many households have no debt, net worth can be negative, and all of these
variables have long right tails. The \scf{} also oversamples wealthy
households, so its weights carry information about wealth.

We make three contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A weighted, sign-gated, chained imputation estimator, available
standalone.} We describe the estimator and its interface contract, and
release it as \populacefit{}, installable and usable on plain data frames
independently of the rest of the \populace{} stack.
\item \textbf{The first application of the population-view harness.} The
harness \citep{ghenis2026popdgp} scores a candidate file against held-out
samples of the surveys that observe the same population. We use it to
compare \cps{} households carrying imputed wealth with held-out \scf{}
households, on demographics and wealth together, and we report where its
metrics separate the methods and where they do not.
\item \textbf{A benchmark that attributes results to each design choice, plus
a reweighting stress test.} We compare the estimator against unweighted and
weighted quantile regression forests \citep{meinshausen2006quantile},
ordinary least squares imputation \citep{vonhippel2013should}, linear
quantile regression \citep{koenker1978regression}, and distance
hot-deck statistical matching, with the donor chosen with and without its
weight \citep{andridge2010review, dorazio2006statistical}, under a paired,
repeated holdout protocol. Ablations switch off one design choice at a time,
and gating and chaining together, so that each choice and the interaction of
those two can be read. A closed-form fragility diagnostic
measures each method's worst-case single-record exposure under bounded
reweightings of the imputed file (the landmine problem).
\end{enumerate}

One policy use shows why the joint distribution matters. Supplemental
Security Income conditions eligibility on countable resources
\citep{ssa2024ssi}, and the \cps{} does not observe wealth. Simulating the
program's baseline caseload on the \cps{}, or a reform such as the SSI
Savings Penalty Elimination Act, which would raise the resource limits
\citep{ssi_spea_2025}, therefore requires imputed wealth, and the
composition of the simulated caseload depends on how imputed wealth varies
with demographics. This paper does not simulate the SSI caseload, and it
does not score fit near the resource limits. Its population-view experiment
(Section~\ref{sec:task-harness}) applies the harness of
\citet{ghenis2026popdgp} to a broader question: whether households assembled
from \cps{} demographics and imputed wealth can be told apart from households
the \scf{} observed.

The rest of the paper proceeds as follows. Section~2 situates the estimator in
the survey-imputation literature. Section~3 specifies the estimator, the
ablation design, the metrics, and the protocol. Section~4 describes the four
benchmark tasks and their data. Section~5 reports results, Section~6
discusses them and their limitations, and Section~7 concludes.
