\section*{Abstract}

Microsimulation models impute variables from one survey onto another. A
model fitted without design weights learns the sample's conditional
distributions, not the population's, where weights are informative. We present \populacefit{}, \policyengine{}'s imputation
estimator: a quantile regression forest that resamples its training data by
weight, models zero, negative, and positive values separately (sign gates),
and imputes targets in sequence (chaining). We compare it with regression
and hot-deck baselines, switching off one design choice at a time, within
the Survey of Consumer Finances (SCF) and the Current Population Survey
(CPS), and across them with the population-view harness, scoring CPS
households with imputed wealth against held-out SCF households. On two SCF
waves, fitting without weights is 4.3 to 6.1 times worse on net-worth
fit and yields a 99th percentile 1.8 to 2.3 times the holdout's,
which the joint metrics miss. The full estimator reproduces the
share of adults without dividend income to within 0.6 percentage points,
against 7.2 with its gates off. Imputing four balance-sheet items
independently costs 4.9 to 5.8 points of classifier AUC, and
computing net worth from the other three removes that cost. A hot deck that
draws donors by weight matches the estimator on within-survey marginal fit
and scores within about one noise standard deviation of it on the joint metrics. The
estimator's imputed 99th percentile is 23 to 37 percent below the
holdout's. Under rules fixed before the second wave was used, six of eight
headline findings replicated, two were inconclusive, and five of nineteen
others failed.

\vspace{1em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} imputation, survey weights, quantile regression
forests, statistical matching
