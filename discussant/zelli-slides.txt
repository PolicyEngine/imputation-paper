===== SLIDE 2 =====
Context & Motivation
Policy needs: Analysis of national legislation increasingly demands answers at
sub-national geographies (States, Congressional Districts).
Data limitation: National surveys are rarely designed to support direct small-area
estimation (sample sizes thin down to a few hundred households). Lack of a single survey
that contains all the variables required to the analysis and to run microsimulation models.
Dimensional paradox: Microsimulation models require joint distributions rich enough to
simulate e.g. complex tax rules, but files must remain small enough to run interactively.

===== SLIDE 3 =====
Twofold Microdata Challenge
The FILL Problem
No single survey observes all required policy variables together.
Imagine you want to test a new tax law. Survey A (e.g. a general household survey) tells
you how many children a family has and what their jobs are, but not its financial profile.
Survey B (e.g. a wealth survey) tells you exactly how much money a family has in the
bank, but not its family structure.
Need to data fusion procedure to “glue” the missing information from survey B (“donor”)
into survey A (“recipient”), creating complete family profiles.
The REPRESENT Problem
Reduce the dataset size to a “manageable” dimension.
Weight the enhanced file so it can reproduce granular administrative aggregates.
Hierarchical & nested consistency constraints (National → State → District).

===== SLIDE 4 =====
Solution Proposed
MICROCOSM (PolicyEngine) — An open-source microdata pipeline articulated in two
stages.
1 Stage 1 offers a compelling case study in modern data fusion , repositioning the classical
statistical matching problem within a non-parametric machine learning framework.
The empirical analysis fuses two primary data sources to create a comprehensive
household dataset.
The process involves a recipient dataset (which has the household demographic data) and
a donor dataset (which has rich financial details).
2 Stage 2 reduces the comprehensive dataset to a smaller certified release, ensuring that
the remaining households match administrative aggregates (targets) across the country.

===== SLIDE 5 =====
Stage 1: Design-Aware Imputation Model I
The Recipient dataset: The US Census Bureau’s Current Population Survey (CPS
ASEC, public-use file), enhanced by PolicyEngine (eCPS) by pooling three ASEC vintages
(2022–2024, ageing the two earlier vintages forward to the latest year): 337,704 records.
It provides a highly representative sample of the US population with detailed information
on demographics, labor supply, and cash incomes, but it lacks comprehensive data on
wealth and household balance sheets.
The Donor dataset: The Survey of Consumer Finances (SCF), administered by the
Federal Reserve.
The Bridging variables (Shared Features): The block of predictor variables common to
both datasets. These include:
Demographics: Age, educational attainment, marital status, and household size.
Income streams: Wage income, self-employment income, capital gains, interest/dividend
income, and social security benefits.

===== SLIDE 6 =====
Stage 1: Design-Aware Imputation Model II
The Imputed variables (The Targets): The goal of Stage 1 is to predict and inject the
missing SCF financial variables into every household profile in the eCPS.
The “glue”: Quantile Regression Forest (QRF) . The QFR acts as a predictive model
that estimates the full distribution of potential target values, rather than a single average.
By retaining the entire data distribution within each decision tree node, the model
identifies specific percentiles—such as the 10th, 50th, or 90th—to simulate realistic
population variations.
Why a ML approach? Traditional data fusion methods like nearest-neighbor hot-deck
excel at maintaining the integrity of real-world data, including accurate outliers and zero
values, because they copy existing records rather than creating synthetic data. However,
these methods fail when using multiple, complex predictors (the curse of dimensionality),
as finding exact matches across many variables becomes impossible. There is a trade-off
between accurately preserving real data and managing complex, multi-dimensional
datasets.

===== SLIDE 7 =====
Stage 1: Design-Aware Imputation Model I
Three crucial improvements of MICROCOSM model to overcome standard Regression Forests
problems:
Weighted Bootstrap: Resamples training rows with replacement proportional to survey
weights before growing trees. Corrects the “weight-blindness” flaw (Standard machine
learning models ignore these weights).
The “Gate” for Zeros (Regime Gates): Economic data is full of zeros. If you use a
standard model to predict wealth, it tends to average things out. It might give everyone a
tiny amount, completely wiping out the zeros. The model uses a regime gate. (In an
econometric world: a selection model). The first step decides if the answer should be zero
or not zero. If it decides ”not zero,” a second forest calculates the exact amount.

===== SLIDE 8 =====
Stage 1: Design-Aware Imputation Model II
Sequential Chaining: Making the Balance Sheets Make Sense. If you have to guess
multiple financial details for the same household (e.g., its debts, its assets, and its net
worth), you cannot guess them completely separately. The model predicts the variables in
a chain (one after the other). First, it guesses debt; then, conditioning on the debt, it
guesses assets; finally, it calculates net worth based on the previous answers. This ensures
accounting identities.

Stage 1: Comments I
Major strength: It demonstrates that ignoring survey weights degrades net worth
(Wasserstein) distance sixfold. Injected bootstrap successfully resolves this.
The non-extrapolation trap: Quantile Regression Forests cannot extrapolate beyond
training support boundaries. As a result, the model understates the q99 extreme tail by
20–30%. Possible to patch the QRF tree model with parametric Pareto tail distributions
to capture the missing 20–30% top-percentile wealth mass?
Comparisons: Empirically comparing the stage 1 imputation model with traditional
hot-deck imputation would clearly justify the use of a more complex machine learning
approach.

===== SLIDE 12 =====
Stage 2: The administrative targets
The 32,633 administrative targets represent the total number of fiscal, socioeconomic, and
demographic aggregates (compiled from official United States institutional databases) used to
calibrate the microsimulation model.
These 32,633 targets are macro-level data points nested across three geographic layers in
the United States. This “surface” consists of:
24,340 congressional-district targets (individual electoral districts).
7,815 state-level targets.
478 national-level targets.
These aggregates come from authoritative administrative sources. The vast majority
—31,350 targets— come from the IRS Statistics of Income (SOI). The remaining targets
are drawn from the Census Bureau (population estimates) and registries of public
assistance programs (such as Medicaid, SNAP/food stamps, and TANF).
They mix count constraints (e.g., the number of program recipients in a specific district)
and dollar amount constraints (e.g., total tax revenue or expenditures).

===== SLIDE 13 =====
Stage 2: Target-Informed Sparse Selection
The targets serve three core purposes:
1 Ensuring sub-national accuracy (Spatial Reweighting): The model shifts and fine-tunes
household weights so that the microdata aggregates scale up to match known, local
administrative reality simultaneously at the national, state, and district levels.
2 Driving smart data pruning (Target-Informed Selection): Instead of dropping records at
random, the pipeline uses a sparse optimization method. The algorithm actively evaluates
the records against the 32,633 targets: a specific household record survives the pruning
process only if keeping it directly helps the model ”hit” a target that would otherwise be
missed.
3 Enabling highly detailed policy simulation : Because the final dataset is tightly
calibrated against this massive surface of real-world benchmarks, MICROCOSM can
accurately simulate the impact of new tax and benefit legislation (such as the One Big
Beautiful Bill Act analyzed in a companion paper) down to the specific grain of
individual states and congressional districts.

===== SLIDE 14 =====
Stage 2: The Optimization Objective (Loss Function)
The optimizer minimizes a multi-term objective function ( L) to balance matching the targets,
keeping the file small, and avoiding erratic weights:
L = Lcal + λL0L0 + λL2L2
Lcal (Calibration Loss): This measures how close the simulated totals are to the real
administrative targets. It uses a metric called capped weighted MAPE (Mean Absolute
Percentage Error). It calculates relative error (so a 1% miss on a small target costs the
same as a 1% miss on a large target) but caps extreme outliers so single hard-to-fit
targets do not corrupt the gradients.
L0 (Sparsity Penalty): This term penalizes the number of active records to force the
dataset to be small.
L2 (Concentration Penalty): This prevents the optimizer from ”cheating” by placing
massive, unrealistic weights on just a few households to hit the targets.

===== SLIDE 15 =====
Stage 2: Solving the L0 Problem: Stochastic Gating
The algorithm is given a double mission. It must minimize the error against real-world
administrative data, but it also gets ”punished” for every household switch (on/off) it
leaves open.
To force the algorithm to actually use those switches and delete records, the authors add
a penalty called the L0 norm, which counts non-zero entries in a vector.
However, the L0 norm is non-differentiable, meaning you cannot use standard gradient
descent to solve it because you cannot calculate its derivatives.
To bypass this, the authors implement Hard Concrete gates ( zi ∈ [0, 1]) attached to each
household parameter: Instead of a hard ”yes/no” choice, the gate is treated as a random
variable drawn from a Concrete distribution (a continuous relaxation of a discrete choice).
This ”trick” allows to turn continuous optimization into discrete record selection.
Roberto Zelli Ghenis et al – Discussion 15 / 21
===== SLIDE 16 =====
Stage 2: The Post-Selection Refit
The weights generated while the switches are turning on and off are not very accurate.
The file gets a high error score of 9.86%.
The authors separate the process into two steps. First, they use the gates purely as a
filter to choose the best support subset of households (57,240 record). Second, lock that
selection in, throw away the stochastic gates, and run a standard dense Adam optimizer
for 1,500 epochs to smoothly refit ordinary calibration weights onto those selected records.
This drops the final error to an impressive 4.74%.

Stage 2: Comments I
The gated weight paradox . The authors find that the continuous weights generated
directly during the joint optimization loop yield a poor objective loss of nearly 10%. To
fix this, they discard those weights and run an independent, secondary post-selection refit
exclusively on the chosen support. If the continuous relaxed gates are poor weight-setters
on their own, how confident can we be that they are truly selecting the optimal structural
support? Does this behavior imply that a lasso alternative or a discrete combinatorial
search might identify an even stronger subset?
Prove the L0 superiority. Section 6.6 of the paper critiques L1 (LASSO)
soft-thresholding shrinkage, but no L1 empirical arm is actually run in the benchmarks.
Run a proximal gradient L1 comparison to numerically justify using the complex Hard
Concrete distribution.

===== SLIDE 20 =====
Stage 2: Comments II
Single-Seed vulnerability: High-stakes numbers on the full target surface are derived
from a single fixed random seed configuration. Is it possible to replace single-seed results
with multi-seed variance blocks to demonstrate that the support selection is statistically
stable?
Weight inflation (Representative Fragility). Because the optimizer forces a small number
of records (57,240) to match tens of thousands of targets, individual household weights
skyrocket up to 913,836. Having one household represent nearly a million families, does it
make the simulator volatile to minor policy tweaks?
Is compressing the dataset down to 57,240 records actually necessary for modern
microsimulation?. The authors argue that a smaller file is required for computational
efficiency and to run interactive microsimulations. In an era of rapid development in
processing power, wouldn’t it be far more methodologically sound to simulate policy rules
directly on a full, unreduced dataset of 337,000 households, or even larger data sets,
